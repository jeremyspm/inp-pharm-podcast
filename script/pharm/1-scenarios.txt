Q: Scenario one. Mrs Harris is eighty-one and lives in aged residential care. She has asthma, atrial fibrillation and osteoarthritis. She's prescribed a salbutamol inhaler, a hundred micrograms, two puffs P R N; warfarin three milligrams P O nocte; metoprolol forty-seven point five milligrams P O mane; and paracetamol one gram P O Q I D. She takes nine medicines in all.

Before the questions, read the chart like Joan wants. P R N, as required. P O, by mouth. Nocte, at night. Mane, in the morning. Q I D, four times a day.

Q: Two marks. What drug class is salbutamol, and how does it work?

Salbutamol is a bronchodilator. It's a beta-2 agonist: it binds to the beta-2 receptors in the smooth muscle of the airways and relaxes it, so the airways open and the wheeze eases. Two marks, two lines.

Q: Two marks. Identify two assessments the nurse makes before and after Mrs Harris uses her salbutamol.

Her respiratory rate, her work of breathing and her wheeze. And her oxygen saturation, or her heart rate, because salbutamol can cause a fast heart rate and a tremor. Before tells you why she needs it. After tells you if it worked.

Q: Two marks. Warfarin is an anticoagulant. Identify one test used to monitor it, and one sign of bleeding you'd teach her to report.

The test is the I N R, a blood test of how long the blood takes to clot. Signs of bleeding: unusual bruising, bleeding gums, blood in her urine, or black stools.

Q: Two marks. Define polypharmacy, and give one strategy to support Mrs Harris with her medicines.

Polypharmacy is taking many medicines at once, often five or more, which raises the risk of interactions, side effects and errors. A strategy: blister packs or a pill organiser with visual aids. That was the answer on her formative two. Or ask for a medication review.

Q: One mark. Identify one age-related change that may mean Mrs Harris needs a lower dose.

Decreased renal function. Her kidneys excrete drugs more slowly, so they build up. That's her formative two, question one, word for word.

{recap} Scenario one, Mrs Harris. Salbutamol: a bronchodilator, a beta-2 agonist that relaxes airway smooth muscle. Assess respiratory rate, wheeze, saturations and heart rate, before and after. Warfarin: monitor the I N R; report bruising, bleeding gums, blood in urine, black stools. Polypharmacy: many medicines at once; blister packs and visual aids. Older adults: decreased renal function.

Q: Scenario two. Leo is four and weighs eighteen kilograms. He has a fever and an ear infection. His G P prescribes amoxicillin oral suspension, and paracetamol fifteen milligrams per kilogram, by mouth, every four to six hours as required, no more than four doses in twenty-four hours. His mum, Ana, says she thinks he might be a bit allergic to antibiotics.

Q: One mark. Calculate Leo's single dose of paracetamol.

Fifteen milligrams times eighteen kilograms is two hundred and seventy milligrams. No working needed, just the answer.

Q: Two marks. Identify two reasons Leo is given paracetamol.

It's an analgesic: it relieves mild to moderate pain, here his sore ear. And it's an antipyretic: it brings his fever down.

Q: One mark. Why are children prescribed lower doses than adults?

Their liver and kidneys are immature, so they metabolise and excrete drugs more slowly. Liver, metabolism. Kidneys, excretion. Joan said this comes up in State Finals too.

Q: Two marks. Ana says Leo might be a bit allergic to antibiotics. What is an allergic reaction, and what should the nurse do?

An allergic reaction is when the body sees the medication as an antigen and mounts an immune response against it. That's her formative one key. The nurse asks exactly what happened last time, documents it, and tells the prescriber before any penicillin is given. Antibiotics cause the most allergic reactions of any drug class.

Q: Two marks. Give one piece of safe advice for Ana about storing and giving amoxicillin suspension.

This is her formative one, question nineteen, and any one answer earns the marks. Store it in the fridge, out of Leo's reach. Or: finish the whole course, even when he seems better, to fully treat the infection. Or: measure it with a medicine syringe or cup, not a kitchen teaspoon.

{recap} Scenario two, Leo. Paracetamol fifteen milligrams per kilogram times eighteen is two hundred and seventy milligrams. Paracetamol is an analgesic and an antipyretic. Children get lower doses because of an immature liver and kidneys. An allergy is the body treating the drug as an antigen; ask, document, tell the prescriber. Amoxicillin: fridge, out of reach, finish the course, use a syringe.

Q: Scenario three. Mr Tane is sixty-four, on the surgical ward after a bowel resection. He has angina and uses a G T N spray. He's prescribed morphine two point five milligrams I V P R N for severe pain, and his own G T N spray, four hundred micrograms, one spray S L, sublingual, P R N for chest pain.

Q: Two marks. Morphine is a controlled drug. What is a controlled drug, and give one way it's handled differently on the ward.

A controlled drug is a medicine under special legal controls, under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1975, to reduce misuse and abuse. That's her formative two key. On the ward it's kept in a double-locked safe, recorded in the controlled drug register, and checked and signed by two nurses. Remember: every controlled drug is prescribed, but not every prescribed drug is controlled.

Q: Two marks. Identify two things the nurse monitors after giving Mr Tane morphine.

His respiratory rate and his level of sedation, because respiratory depression is morphine's toxic effect. And his pain score, to see if it worked. You could also say blood pressure, nausea, or constipation.

Q: Two marks. Why is G T N given sublingually, and what's one side effect?

Under the tongue, it's absorbed straight into the bloodstream, so it works very fast and it skips the first-pass metabolism in the liver. Angina is dangerous: the heart muscle is short of oxygen, so speed matters. A side effect: headache, dizziness, or low blood pressure, which is why he sits down before he uses it.

Q: One mark. Mr Tane says he doesn't want his next dose. What should the nurse do?

Respect it: a person can legally refuse a medicine. Find out why, document the refusal, and tell the R N or the prescriber. True, and document it, was her formative one answer.

{recap} Scenario three, Mr Tane. Controlled drug: under special legal controls to reduce misuse and abuse; double-locked safe, register, two nurses. After morphine, monitor respiratory rate, sedation and pain. G T N is sublingual for speed and to skip first-pass; headache and low blood pressure, so sit down. A refusal: respect it, ask why, document, report.
