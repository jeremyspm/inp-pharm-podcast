# How this forecast works
@ why: Joan has published three formative mini-exams but not the real paper. This track sets out what she said the paper is, and how the rest of the episode was chosen from it.
@ say: How this forecast works

Kia ora. This is the Pharm final, the forecast. The exam is Thursday the fifth of November: eighty minutes, and the reading time is inside those eighty minutes. Forty-one questions, fifty marks. Thirty multi-choice at one mark each, then eleven short answers worth twenty marks between them, from one mark to four, sitting under three clinical scenarios.

Here's what Joan told us, and it shapes every answer in this episode.

One. A short answer is a definition plus an example. Her one worked example: what is the meaning of contraindication, and give an example.

Two. About two lines per mark. When a student asked about writing a hundred and fifty words, she said, no, not a hundred and fifty. That's the I N P rule, not hers. Simple, concise, to the point. Identify and describe means name it, then describe it in a few words. Don't just write the word.

Three. It's applied and nurse-focused. What do you assess before you give the drug? What do you monitor? What do you say when the patient asks you something? Her words: it's not a pharmacy exam.

Four. The drugs. On her exam-prep slides, under medications with specific questions, she names two: paracetamol and salbutamol. In class on the twentieth she added the classes she's taught: aspirin, warfarin and heparin; G T N; beta blockers; the penicillins; and morphine. Maybe one question a drug, she said, so don't spend a day on G T N.

Five. Abbreviations are simple marks, maybe five of them. And a couple of easy calculations as multi-choice, no working shown.

And the modules on her list: concepts and drug names, calculations, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, administration and patient education, the lifespan, vaccines and allergy, licit and illicit drugs, drug development, and complementary medicine with the socioeconomic factors.

So here's the order. First, three forecast scenarios, built the way her formatives are: a named patient, then four or five short questions. Then a track for each drug she named. Then the principles, module by module. Then her own formative keys, and the rapid-fire track.

The examiner, this voice,

Q: reads each question, with its marks,

and I answer it in her shape. Pause after the question if you want to try it first.
